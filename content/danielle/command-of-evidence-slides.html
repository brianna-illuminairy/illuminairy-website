Command of Evidence

Every one of these questions gives you a claim and asks you to pick the evidence that fits it. Most wrong answers come from misreading what the question is actually asking for, so this lesson starts there, then builds the habit of deciding your answer before you read the choices.

Start here every time

Read the question carefully before you look for the answer

These questions are worded in different ways, and the wording tells you two things: which idea in the passage you are working with, and whether you support it or argue against it. Read the wrong idea, or miss the word "undermine," and you will pick a choice that does the opposite of what was asked.

"Which quotation from the article best illustrates Bloom's argument?"
Match one named person's argument, Bloom's, not the other expert in the passage.
"Which finding would most strongly support the researchers' hypothesis?"
Match the hypothesis, the specific thing they predicted, and support it.
"Which finding would most directly undermine the conclusion of the study?"
Match the conclusion, then find what argues against it, not for it.
"Which choice best uses data from the table to complete the statement?"
Match the sentence with the blank, and fill it in the direction that sentence is going.

A difference that decides where to look

Some questions want words from the passage; others want an outside fact that fits it

When the question asks for a quotation, the answer is text that already lives in the passage or a work it names, so you go find it. When the question asks which finding "if true" would support or undermine an idea, the answer is a new fact you have never seen, and your only job is to judge whether it would help or hurt the idea.

Look inside the passage: "Which quotation illustrates the claim?" The right words are already there.
Look outside the passage: "Which finding, if true, would support the hypothesis?" First read the hypothesis in the passage, then judge each new fact by whether it would back that hypothesis up.
Look at the figure and the sentence: "Which choice completes the statement using the graph?" The numbers come from the figure, but the sentence decides which numbers are right.

A method that works on all of them

If the passage says A and the question asks about B, decide what the answer must do

1Read the question and find what it is asking for, and where to look. A hypothesis, one person's argument, the overall claim, the conclusion, or something that argues against one of these.
2Restate that idea in your own words, using only what the passage says. "The hypothesis is ...", "Bloom's argument is ...", "the claim is ...".
3Decide what the answer must do to that idea: support it, complete it, or argue against it.
4Find the choice that does it. If it is obvious, pick it. Cross out choices only when the answer does not jump out.
Step 2 is where people get confused. Restate the idea exactly as the passage puts it. If you find yourself adding a detail to make it fit better, stop, because the answer matches the passage's idea, not a sharper version you built.

Trap 1 of 4

More than one choice can be true, and only one answers the question

The most common mistake is picking a choice because it is true and you can find it in the passage, even though it does not do what the question asked. Several choices are often true. Being true is not the test. Doing the job the question asked is.

On the next screen, sort the choices first. Mark each one true or false against the passage, notice that more than one is true, then see which of the true ones actually supports the claim.

Trap 2 of 4

A choice can be about the right topic but miss what the claim actually says

Read what the claim asserts, exactly. If the claim is that something is harmful, a choice that only shows the thing exists is about the right topic but does not show harm. If the claim is about a total, a choice about one part does not cover it.

Claim: avocado monoculture introduces problems, meaning it is harmful.
Trap choice: land once used for mixed crops is now planted only with avocado.
Why it fails: that shows monoculture exists, not that it causes harm. The claim is about harm, so the answer has to show a problem.

Trap 3 of 4

A choice can point the opposite way, especially on undermine questions

If the question asks you to undermine and a choice supports the idea, it is wrong no matter how well it fits. This is the trap in the glial cells question: three of the four choices are about the effect of glutamate, and the one that restates the passage's own result supports the conclusion instead of arguing against it.

Question: which finding would undermine the claim that a drug improves memory?
Trap choice: people taking the drug scored higher on memory tests.
Why it fails: that supports the claim. The question asked you to weaken it, so you need the finding that shows no effect.

Trap 4 of 4

Two things happening together does not prove one caused the other

When a claim is about cause, a choice that only shows two things rising at the same time is not enough. This shows up most in data questions.

Claim: the ad campaign caused the rise in sales.
Trap choice: sales and the campaign both rose in the same quarter.
Why it fails: rising together is only timing. Support has to tie the campaign to the sales, the way heat, not ice cream, drives summer sunburns.

Type 1 · quotation questions

Match the quotation to the exact claim, and to the right person

The choices are quotations, and the right one shows the claim is true. When a passage quotes more than one person, the question names whose argument you want, and the other person's view is planted as a trap.

Before the choices, say the claim in your own words and note whose it is. On the next screen, the passage holds two opposing experts, so whose argument you are illustrating decides the answer.

A student asserts that the production of these diverse creations by others reflects Kurosawa's own approach to crafting film.

Hint
Explanation

2A is the trap most students pick. It is true that scholars analyzed his films, but scholars studying him is not his approach being reflected in other people's work. True but does not support the claim.

Type 2 · finding, if true

The answer is usually a new fact, not something from the passage

These say "which finding, if true." The choices are hypothetical results you are asked to imagine. The right one does not appear in the passage; it is an outside fact that would support or undermine the idea the question names.

This flips the usual instinct. On quotation questions you hunt for text that is already there. On finding questions you accept a choice you have never seen and ask only one thing: if this were true, would it make the claim stronger, or weaker, exactly as the question asked?

Raffaelli notes the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood.
Which finding from the study, if true, would most strongly support the researchers' hypothesis?

Hint
Explanation

Passage
Researchers concluded that online and offline learning are distinct processes governed by different cellular mechanisms. Glial cells release gliotransmitters like glutamate, which can influence memory. In mice, activating glial cells enhanced online learning, while inhibiting them suppressed it.
Which finding, if true, would most directly undermine the conclusion of the study?
Select an answer, then check it or open the explanation.
Hint
Explanation
1The conclusion is that online and offline learning run on different mechanisms, and glutamate is the online one. To undermine that, show glutamate also drives offline learning the same way. Choice A shows more glutamate raising offline learning, just as it raises online, so the two are not distinct after all. Choice A.
2C is the trap most students pick. It restates the passage's own result, that glutamate boosts online learning, which supports the conclusion. The question asked you to argue against it, so a supporting choice is wrong.

Explanation

Select an answer, then check it or open the explanation.
Hint
Explanation
1Ferguson says country-specific drivers mattered beyond the shared ones. The way to support that is to show the shared factor alone cannot explain the results. Choice A does it: same Marshall Plan aid, very different growth, so something specific to each country must be at work. Choice A.
2C is the trap most students pick. Uniform resurgence from a standardized shared effort argues the shared factors explain everything, which is the opposite of Ferguson's point. B does the same with a uniform continental average.

Type 3 · data questions

Read the figure before you read the choices

1Read the title, the labels, and the units. Know what each row, column, or axis measures before anything else.
2Read the sentence with the blank and note what it needs: a rise, a fall, a comparison between two groups, or a specific number.
3Find that in the figure yourself, then match it to a choice.
4Check both halves of each choice: the numbers must be right, and they must complete the sentence's point. A choice can quote the figure correctly and still not fit the sentence.

A logic pattern in data claims

"Necessary but not sufficient" asks the data to show two separate things

Necessary means the result never happens without the condition. Every case missing the condition fails to get the result.
Not sufficient means the condition alone does not guarantee the result. At least one case has the condition and still fails.
A choice that shows only one half is incomplete. If a claim says a condition is necessary but not sufficient, the right answer has to reflect both the "never without it" part and the "does not guarantee it" part.

Four quick reads

See both halves in four everyday cases

A key for a locked door. Necessary: you never get in without the key. Not sufficient: the key alone fails if the door is also bolted.
Water for a plant to grow. Necessary: no water, no growth, ever. Not sufficient: watered plants in the dark still die, so water alone does not guarantee growth.
Studying to pass a test. Necessary: students who never study fail. Not sufficient: some students study and still fail, so studying alone does not guarantee a pass.
Rain for a flood. Necessary: no floods occur without rain here. Not sufficient: light rain brings no flood, so rain alone does not guarantee one.
