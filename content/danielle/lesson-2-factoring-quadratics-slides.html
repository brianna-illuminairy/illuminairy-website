Part 1 of 6

Quadratics on the SAT

What they are, the shapes they take, and how the test hides them.

The definition

What is a quadratic?

A quadratic is an expression whose highest power is 2. It has an x² term and nothing higher:

ax² + bx + c

a, b, and c are just numbers, and a is not 0. In x² + 5x + 6, a = 1, b = 5, c = 6.

Expression vs equationThe quadratic above is an expression on its own. It becomes a quadratic equation when it is set equal to something, like y = x² + 5x + 6 or x² + 5x = 6. Solving means finding the x-values that make such an equation true.
How it shows up

The same quadratic, in different forms

One quadratic can be written several ways. The expanded form and the factored form below are the identical quadratic:

  • ✶Expanded: x² + 5x + 6
  • ✶Factored: (x + 2)(x + 3)

The SAT does not always set it equal to zero. It may set it equal to a number, or put terms on both sides of the equals sign:

  • ✶x² + 5x = −6  ·  equal to a number
  • ✶y − 42 = (y − c)(y − 42)  ·  terms on both sides, like Q11
First move when solvingWhatever form it arrives in, the first step to solve is to gather every term onto one side, so the other side is zero.
The traps

What can make a quadratic hard to spot

  • ✶No visible x². It is already in parentheses, like (y − c)(y − 42). The y² only appears if you multiply it out.
  • ✶A letter instead of a number, like the c above. It is still a quadratic in y.
  • ✶A negative in front, like −x² + 81. The leading minus hides a familiar pattern.
  • ✶Only two terms instead of three. 3x² + 7x has no plain number on its own (no c), and x² − 49 has no x term (no b). Both are still quadratics.
Does x² by itself count?Yes. The only requirement is that the highest power is 2. So x², x² − 49, and 3x² + 7x are all quadratics, even with fewer than three terms. A quadratic does not need a middle term or a constant.
The language

The words that signal a quadratic

When a question uses any of these, it is asking about a quadratic, and they all mean the same thing: the x-values that make the equation true.

All the same wordsolutions = roots = zeros = x-intercepts. Four names for the answers you get from factoring.
  • ✶"How many solutions" is asking how many of those x-values exist (usually two, sometimes one, sometimes none).
  • ✶"In the form a + b√n" tells you the answer will not factor cleanly and involves a square root. We cover these radical-answer cases separately.
The plan

How to solve a quadratic equation

Solving means finding the x-values that make the equation true. A question signals this by giving you a quadratic and asking for its solutions, roots, zeros, or x-intercepts. Here is the full process, worked on x² + 5x = 6:

  • Move every term onto one side so the other side is zero: x² + 5x − 6 = 0.
  • Factor the quadratic: (x + 6)(x − 1) = 0.
  • Set each factor equal to zero: x + 6 = 0 and x − 1 = 0.
  • Solve each for x: x = −6 or x = 1. Those are the solutions.

The next slide explains why setting it equal to zero is what unlocks the answers.

The reason it works

The zero product property

Solving by factoring relies on one rule about multiplying and zero:

if a · b = 0, then a = 0 or b = 0

The only way for a product to equal zero is for at least one of the things being multiplied to be zero. Two numbers that are both non-zero can never multiply to zero.

Why this solves the quadraticOnce the equation is factored and set equal to zero, like (x + 2)(x + 3) = 0, the two factors multiply to zero, so one of them must be zero: either x + 2 = 0 or x + 3 = 0. Solving each gives x = −2 or x = −3. That is where both solutions come from.
What factoring is

Factoring, defined

Factoring is the process of breaking down a number, expression, or polynomial into simpler building blocks called factors. It is the reverse of the distributive property, or of ordinary multiplication: when you multiply, you combine smaller parts; when you factor, you break the larger result back down to find those original parts.

One clear way to see itFactoring means dividing every term by the same number, then writing that number outside a set of parentheses.
  • Take 12 + 18. Both terms divide by 6: 12 ÷ 6 = 2 and 18 ÷ 6 = 3.
  • So 12 + 18 = 6(2 + 3). Writing the 6 outside the parentheses is exactly what factoring is.
  • Check by multiplying back: 6 × 2 = 12 and 6 × 3 = 18.

From numbers to expressions

Factoring in algebra

In algebra, factoring means rewriting an expression as a multiplication problem. It usually means pulling out the common elements that every term shares, or spotting a known pattern.

  • Take 2x + 6. Both terms are divisible by 2.
  • Divide each by 2: 2x ÷ 2 = x and 6 ÷ 2 = 3.
  • Write the 2 outside the parentheses: 2(x + 3). Multiply back to check: 2 × x = 2x and 2 × 3 = 6.

It is the same move as with numbers: 12 + 18 = 6(2 + 3) becomes 2x + 6 = 2(x + 3). For a quadratic, the factors come out as two sets of parentheses, each holding an x, like x² + 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3).

Part 3 of 6

The distributive property

Multiplying things out, and why factoring is the exact reverse.

The definition

What the distributive property is

To multiply a number by a sum in parentheses, you multiply it by each term inside, then add the results.

a(b + c) = ab + ac
  • ✶3(x + 5): 3 times x is 3x, 3 times 5 is 15. So 3x + 15.
  • ✶It works with subtraction too: a(b − c) = ab − ac.
The three ways

The three ways you multiply things out

  • ✶Numbers times numbers: 3 · 4 = 12. Plain arithmetic.
  • ✶A term outside times terms inside: x(x + 4) = x² + 4x. The outside x hits each inside term.
  • ✶Two brackets times each other: (x + 2)(x + 3). Every term in the first hits every term in the second, then you combine: x² + 5x + 6.
The one that makes quadraticsThe third way is the important one. Multiplying two brackets is what produces an x² term, which is exactly what a quadratic has.
The connection

How distributing and factoring relate

They are opposite directions of the same thing.

(x + 2)(x + 3)
⇄multiply / factor
x² + 5x + 6
  • ✶Multiplying two brackets makes a quadratic.
  • ✶So factoring a quadratic means finding the two brackets that multiply to give it. You are running the multiplication backward.
Part 4 of 6

How to factor

All the methods, each defined, with the work shown step by step.

A definition you need first

Common factors and the GCF

A common factor is a number that divides evenly into two or more numbers with no remainder. You find them by listing each number's factors and seeing which ones appear in both lists.

  • ✶Factors of 12: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12
  • ✶Factors of 18: 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18
  • ✶Shared by both: 1, 2, 3, 6. These are the common factors.
Greatest common factorThe largest shared factor is the greatest common factor, the GCF. For 12 and 18 it is 6. Factoring a quadratic starts with pulling out the GCF of all its terms.
Method 1 · factor out the GCF

Factor out the greatest common factor

Find the greatest common factor of all the terms, pull it out to the front of a set of parentheses, and put what is left, after dividing each term by it, inside the parentheses. This is the first thing to try on any quadratic.

  • ✶In 3x² + 12x − 36, all three terms divide by 3. Pull the 3 out front and put each remainder inside: 3(x² + 4x − 12).
  • ✶In 2x² − 8x, both terms divide by 2, and both also have an x, so the GCF is 2x. Pull 2x out, leftovers inside: 2x(x − 4).
Method 1 · worked step by step

Factor 2x² + 6x

  • What is the largest number that divides both 2 and 6? It is 2.
  • Does every term contain an x? Yes, so x is part of the common factor too.
  • So the greatest common factor is 2x.
  • Divide each term by the GCF: 2x² ÷ 2x = x, and 6x ÷ 2x = 3.
  • Write 2x in front of the parentheses, and put the leftovers inside: 2x(x + 3).
  • Check by multiplying back: 2x · x = 2x² and 2x · 3 = 6x. It matches the original.
Method 1 · only a number in common

Factor out the common number

  • ✶2x + 6 = 2(x + 3)
  • ✶3x + 12 = 3(x + 4)
  • ✶5x − 10 = 5(x − 2)
  • ✶4x + 8 = 4(x + 2)
Method 1 · a variable in common

Factor out the common x, and watch for the invisible 1

  • ✶x² + 4x = x(x + 4)
  • ✶x² − 7x = x(x − 7)
  • ✶x² + x = x(x + 1) the lone x leaves a 1
  • ✶2x² + x = x(2x + 1) the lone x leaves a 1
The invisible 1When a term is just a single x, dividing it by x leaves 1, not nothing. x ÷ x = 1. Leaving out that 1 is the most common slip here.
Method 1 · a number and a variable in common

Factor out the GCF when it has both

  • ✶2x² + 6x = 2x(x + 3)
  • ✶3x² − 12x = 3x(x − 4)
  • ✶6x² + 9x = 3x(2x + 3) GCF is 3x
  • ✶12x² − 8x = 4x(3x − 2) GCF is 4x
Method 1 · negatives

Pulling out a negative

When the first term is negative, pull out a negative common factor. Doing so flips the sign of each leftover inside.

  • ✶−x² − 3x = −x(x + 3)
  • ✶−2x² + 4x = −2x(x − 2)
  • ✶−3x² − 6x = −3x(x + 2)
Watch the flipIn −2x² + 4x, the +4x divided by −2x becomes −2. That sign flip is why the inside reads (x − 2).
A definition you need first

What is a trinomial?

A trinomial is an expression with exactly three terms. "Tri" means three. x² + 5x + 6 has three terms, so it is a trinomial.

Trinomial vs quadraticQuadratic describes the highest power (a 2). Trinomial describes the number of terms (three). x² + 5x + 6 is both: a quadratic trinomial. They are not the same word.

Not every quadratic is a trinomial. x² − 9 is a quadratic with only two terms. 3x² + 7x is a quadratic with two terms. The method on the next slides is for the three-term kind.

Method 2 · simple trinomial

Find two numbers that fit

For a trinomial x² + bx + c, you need two numbers that do two jobs at once:

  • ✶They multiply to give the last number, c.
  • ✶They add to give the middle number, b.
  • ✶Once you find them, put one in each bracket, right after the x: (x + first)(x + second).
Method 2 · worked step by step

Factor x² + 5x + 6

  • Name the parts: the last number is 6, the middle number is 5.
  • I need two numbers that multiply to 6 and add to 5.
  • List the pairs that multiply to 6: 1 and 6 (they add to 7, no), 2 and 3 (they add to 5, yes).
  • So the two numbers are 2 and 3.
  • Put one in each bracket: (x + 2)(x + 3).
  • Check: x·x = x², x·3 + 2·x = 5x, 2·3 = 6. Matches.
Method 2 · the signs do the searching

The signs tell you which numbers to look for

Before you hunt for the pair, the signs narrow it down so you are not guessing. Read the last sign first.

  • ✶Last positive, middle positive → both numbers positive. x² + 5x + 6
  • ✶Last positive, middle negative → both numbers negative. x² − 5x + 6
  • ✶Last negative → one positive, one negative. The bigger one takes the middle's sign. x² + x − 6
Why this helps factoringKnowing the signs first cuts the list of pairs in half. For x² − 5x + 6 you only check negative pairs, so you go straight to −2 and −3.
Method 2 · build up

Both positive, then both negative

  • ✶x² + 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3)
  • ✶x² + 7x + 12 = (x + 3)(x + 4)
  • ✶x² + 8x + 15 = (x + 3)(x + 5)
  • ✶x² − 5x + 6 = (x − 2)(x − 3)
  • ✶x² − 7x + 12 = (x − 3)(x − 4)
Method 2 · last number negative

One positive, one negative

  • ✶x² + x − 6 = (x + 3)(x − 2) middle is +1x
  • ✶x² − x − 6 = (x − 3)(x + 2) middle is −1x
  • ✶x² + 2x − 15 = (x + 5)(x − 3)
  • ✶x² − 2x − 8 = (x − 4)(x + 2)
  • ✶x² − 5x − 14 = (x − 7)(x + 2) multiply to −14, add to −5
Method 3 · difference of squares

A square minus a square

When you see one square minus another square, with no middle term, it always splits into the same two terms with a plus and a minus.

a² − b² = (a − b)(a + b)
  • Basic: x² − 49. Here a = x and b = 7 (since 7² = 49) → (x − 7)(x + 7).
  • Harder: 4x² − 81. Here a = 2x (since (2x)² = 4x²) and b = 9 → (2x − 9)(2x + 9).
Method 4 · perfect square trinomial

When the trinomial is a bracket times itself

A perfect square trinomial is one that factors into the same bracket twice, so you can write it as that bracket squared. You spot it when the two numbers you find come out equal.

a² + 2ab + b² = (a + b)²  ·  a² − 2ab + b² = (a − b)²
  • x² + 10x + 25: two numbers that multiply to 25 and add to 10 are 5 and 5. Same number, so it is (x + 5)².
  • x² − 12x + 36: multiply to 36, add to −12 → −6 and −6 → (x − 6)².
Method 5 · shared chunk

A common factor can be a whole expression

The common factor does not have to be a number or a single x. When two terms contain the same expression, that whole expression is their common factor. Pull it out front, and combine what is left inside the parentheses, exactly like Method 1.

  • x(x + 2) + 5(x + 2): both terms contain (x + 2), so that is the common factor. Pull it out, leftovers inside: (x + 2)(x + 5).
  • 3(x − 1) + x(x − 1): both contain (x − 1) → (x − 1)(x + 3).
The idea behind Q11Recognizing a repeated expression as a common factor is the whole move. The Q11 walkthrough later is this same step on a harder equation.
Method 5 · shared chunk

When the chunk repeats with a power, rename it

Sometimes the same chunk shows up squared and again on its own, like (x − 3)² and (x − 3). That hides an ordinary quadratic. Renaming the chunk as a single letter (this is called substitution) makes the quadratic visible.

  • Find the chunk that repeats.
  • Let a single letter, like u, equal that chunk.
  • Factor the simple quadratic in u with the methods you already know.
  • Put the chunk back in place of u.
  • Simplify inside each set of parentheses.
Do not expand the chunkNever multiply out (x − 3)² at the start. It is correct math, but it creates a much bigger expression that is far harder to factor. Keep the chunk whole.
Method 5 · worked, step by step

Solve (x − 3)² − 4(x − 3) − 12 = 0

  • The chunk (x − 3) repeats. Let u = (x − 3).
  • The equation becomes u² − 4u − 12 = 0, an ordinary trinomial.
  • Factor it: multiply to −12, add to −4 → −6 and 2 → (u − 6)(u + 2) = 0.
  • Put (x − 3) back for u: (x − 3 − 6)(x − 3 + 2) = 0, which is (x − 9)(x − 1) = 0.
  • Set each factor to zero: x = 9 or x = 1.
Method 5 · a harder version

Factor x⁴ − 7x² + 12

The chunk here is x², because x⁴ is just (x²)². The same substitution works.

  • Let u = x². The expression becomes u² − 7u + 12.
  • Factor: multiply to 12, add to −7 → −3 and −4 → (u − 3)(u − 4).
  • Put x² back for u: (x² − 3)(x² − 4).
  • x² − 4 is a difference of squares, so factor it further: (x² − 3)(x − 2)(x + 2).

The hidden ways

The disguises the SAT uses to make a familiar pattern look new.

Hidden trick · the leading −1

A negative in front, hiding a pattern

When a quadratic starts with a negative term, it is hard to factor as written. Pull out a −1 first. That flips every sign inside and uncovers a pattern you know.

  • Start with −x² + 81.
  • Pull out −1: −1(x² − 81). Notice the signs inside flipped.
  • Inside is now a difference of squares: −1(x − 9)(x + 9).
Hidden trick · the invisible 1

A missing number is really a 1

When the middle is just x or −x with no number, the number is 1. The SAT leaves it off, but you read it as 1 and factor normally.

  • x² − x − 12 is really x² − 1x − 12.
  • Two numbers that multiply to −12 and add to −1: −4 and 3.
  • Factored: (x − 4)(x + 3).
Hidden trick · two parts, both with an x

If both parts have an x, factor the x out

If a quadratic has only two parts and both parts contain an x, you always just factor the x out front.

3x² + 7x = 0  →  x(3x + 7) = 0
Why one answer is always x = 0After factoring, the x sits outside as one factor, and the stuff inside the parentheses is the other factor. By the zero product property, one of them must be zero. The outside x gives x = 0 right away, and the inside gives the other answer. So x = 0 is always one of the two solutions in this case.
Worked, step by step · easy to hard

Both parts have an x

  • x² + 9x = 0 → factor out x → x(x + 9) = 0 → x = 0 or x = −9.
  • 5x² − 10x = 0 → x(5x − 10) = 0 → x = 0, or 5x − 10 = 0 → x = 0 or x = 2.
  • −3x² + 12x = 0 → x(−3x + 12) = 0 → x = 0, or −3x + 12 = 0 → x = 0 or x = 4.

The front number does not change the method. You still factor out the x, x = 0 is always one answer, and the inside gives the other.

Reversed subtraction · the intuition

The temperature trick

Flipping the order of a subtraction never changes the number, it only flips the sign. Two quick questions show why:

  • The temperature drops from 10 degrees to 7. What is the change? 7 − 10 = −3.
  • The temperature rises from 7 degrees to 10. What is the change? 10 − 7 = +3.
The breakthroughSame number, 3, both times. Only the direction flipped. Reversing the order of a subtraction keeps the size of the answer and only switches its sign from positive to negative.
Reversed subtraction · the rule

Switch and slap

When you see a subtraction bracket in the order you do not want, you can flip it, but it costs a negative sign out front.

  • Spot it: you see (5 − x), but you wish it read (x − 5).
  • Switch it: rewrite the order to match: (x − 5).
  • Slap it: put a negative sign outside the bracket to pay for the switch: −(x − 5).
(5 − x)
=switch & slap
−(x − 5)
Prove it with a numberLet x = 2. Then (5 − 2) = 3, and −(2 − 5) = −(−3) = 3. Same answer, so (5 − x) = −(x − 5) really is true.
Hidden trick · reversed subtraction

A flipped subtraction is a hidden −1

When two brackets look almost the same but the subtraction is reversed, one is just −1 times the other.

5 − x = −(x − 5)
  • ✶So a fraction like (x − 5) / (5 − x) is (x − 5) / −(x − 5), which simplifies to −1.
  • ✶Anytime you see (a − b) and (b − a) in the same problem, one is the negative of the other.
Hidden trick · the invisible 1

Where the 1 comes from when you factor

When you pull out a common factor, each term inside the parentheses answers one question: the common factor times what gives this term? That "what" is the leftover. When a term is already equal to the common factor, the answer is 1, because anything times 1 is itself.

  • Factor x out of x² + x. Ask of each term: x times what?
  • x² is x times x. And x is x times 1, since x · 1 = x.
  • So the leftovers are x and 1: x(x + 1).
How to spot itThe 1 was always there. A term written as just x is really 1x, and a bare bracket like (y − 42) is really 1(y − 42). The cue is a term with no number in front and nothing else attached to it. Write its 1 in before you factor, and you will not drop it. That is the first step in Q11.
Hidden trick · the invisible 1

Protect the empty spot with a 1

When a term is just the common factor with nothing else attached, it is naked. You put a 1 in front of it to hold its spot, so that when you pull the common factor out, there is still something left behind. A term that already has another factor sitting next to it (a roommate) does not need a 1, because the roommate is holding the spot.

In subtraction, nothing leaves 0. In factoring, nothing leaves 1.
Say it in three steps1. Identify: both terms share the same block. 2. Protect: if a term has nothing else attached to that block, put a 1 in front to hold its spot. 3. Pull out: pull the block to the front, open one big set of parentheses, and copy what each term has left.
Your turn · find the naked block

Put the guard 1 where it belongs

Same block in both terms. Whichever term has nothing else attached gets the 1.

Left side naked
(x + 7) + (x + 7)(x − 5)
Both share (x + 7). The left term is naked, so guard it: 1(x + 7) + (x + 7)(x − 5)
Pull out: (x + 7)[1 + (x − 5)]
Right side naked
(2x + 3)(x + 4) − (x + 4)
Both share (x + 4). The right term is naked, so guard it: (2x + 3)(x + 4) − 1(x + 4)
Pull out: (x + 4)[(2x + 3) − 1]

Solving, and the real Q11

Putting factoring to work, one step at a time.

The finish

Factor, then set each factor to zero

x² + 5x + 6 = 0
  • Factor: (x + 2)(x + 3) = 0.
  • Set each factor to zero: x + 2 = 0 and x + 3 = 0.
  • Solve each: x = −2 and x = −3. Those are your two solutions.
The Q11 skill

A quadratic does not always show an x²

This is the exact step Danielle missed. A quadratic is often already in parentheses, so you never see x² written out.

The recognition ruleIf you see two things in parentheses multiplied together, and each one has a variable in it, it is a quadratic. Multiplying them would create the squared term.
  • ✶(x + 2)(x + 3) multiplies out to x² + 5x + 6. Quadratic.
  • ✶(y − c)(y − 42) has a y² hiding in it, even with the letter c. Quadratic. This is the right side of Q11.
A trap to avoid

Do not multiply the common bracket back open

When two terms contain the same bracket, that bracket is their common factor. Multiplying it open throws the common factor away and makes more work. Compare both paths on x(x + 3) + (x + 3):

✗ Expanding
x(x + 3) + (x + 3)
= x² + 3x + x + 3
= x² + 4x + 3
The common factor is gone. Now you are factoring from scratch.
✓ Leaving it
x(x + 3) + 1(x + 3)
The common factor is (x + 3).
= (x + 3)(x + 1)
One step.
Her Q11 · factoring it

Factoring is division: divide every term by the common factor

(y − 42) − (y − c)(y − 42) = 0

The common factor is (y − 42). Factoring it out means dividing every term by it, so write each term over (y − 42):

(y − 42)(y − 42) − (y − c)(y − 42)(y − 42) = 0
  • On the right, the (y − 42) on top and bottom cancel, leaving (y − c).
  • On the left, (y − 42) over itself. A thing divided by itself is 1, not 0. If it were 0, the whole left term would vanish, which cannot be right. So it leaves 1.
  • The leftovers are 1 and (y − c). Put (y − 42) back out front: (y − 42)[1 − (y − c)] = 0.
Her Q11 · solving it

Set the whole thing to zero

(y − 42)[1 − (y − c)] = 0
  • First factor: y − 42 = 0 → y = 42.
  • Second factor: 1 − (y − c) = 0. Open the bracket: 1 − y + c = 0, so y = c + 1.
The exact trapOpening −(y − c) flips both signs to −y + c, which gives c + 1. The test plants c − 1 in the choices to catch a sign slip. The answer is c + 1.
How to be sure

Multiply back to check

Factoring and multiplying are opposites, so to check any factoring, multiply it back out and see if you land on the original.

(x + 2)(x + 3)
→multiply back
x² + 5x + 6

It matches what you started with, so the factoring is right. Do this whenever you are unsure.

Practice challenge

What is the positive solution?

−2x² − 2x + 24 = 0
  • The leading term is negative and every term is even, so the common factor is −2. Pull it out: −2(x² + x − 12) = 0.
  • Factor the trinomial: multiply to −12, add to 1 → 4 and −3 → −2(x + 4)(x − 3) = 0.
  • Set each factor to zero: x = −4 or x = 3.
  • The positive solution is x = 3.
✶ The whole routine

How it shows up, and what to do

  • ✶Recognize it is a quadratic, even with no visible x², a letter, a negative, or a missing term.
  • ✶Pull out any common factor first.
  • ✶Factor the rest: a trinomial pair, a difference of squares, a perfect square, or a shared chunk.
  • ✶Set each factor to zero and read off both answers. Watch the signs.
